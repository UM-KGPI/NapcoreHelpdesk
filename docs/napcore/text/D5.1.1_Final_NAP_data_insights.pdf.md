1. Introduction
The Intelligent Transport Systems (ITS) Directive 2010/40/EU (consolidated version including the
amendments introduced by Directive 2023/2661/EU) on the framework for the deployment of ITS in
the field of road transport and for interfaces with other modes of transport establishes, under Article
17, an obligation for Member States to report to the European Commission by 21 March 2025 and on
a 3 -yearly basis thereafter, on the progress made in the implementation of the Directive and its
Delegated Regulations and implementing acts [1]. This report covers the main national activities and
projects related to established priority areas and the availability of data and services listed in Annexes
III and IV of the Directive . This obligation creates a recurring need for Member States to maintain
structured, consistent, and reliable information on the availability and provision of their mobility data.
Within the scope of the NAPCORE initiative, through which the European Commission seeks to support
Member States to better implement the goals of the ITS Directive, Task 5.1, NAP Data Insights &
Reporting Support , aims at identifying more effective and coherent approaches to fulfilling their
reporting obligations under the ITS Directive. National Access Points (NAPs) were very early on
identified as a strategic source of information for future reporting activities.
Figure 1 conceptually illustrates Task 5.1. The storyboard starts with the reporting obligations defined
by the ITS Directive, under which Member States must submit an implementation report to the
European Commission by 21 March 2025 and subsequently provide progress reports every three years.
It then depicts the various stakeholders involved in collecting, preparing and analysing the information
required for reporting. NAPCORE, represented in yellow (bottom -left frame), through research and
development activities, intends to su pport compliance with the formal reporting requirements and
streamline the methodologies and assessment strategies used for reporting, improving its
completeness, ease of implementation and harmonisation across Member States.

1.1. Background on National Access Points
NAPs, as defined by the ITS Directive 2010/40/EU, serve as single points of access to the national
mobility data ecosystem in each Member State. These digital interfaces play a central role in making
mobility data discoverable, accessible, and reusable, thereb y supporting both national and cross -
border ITS services [1].
NAPCORE refines the definition as digital interfaces set up by a Member State to provide a single-point
access to data, in compliance with the Delegated Regulations of the European Commission that
establish functional, technical, organisational, and service provisions to guarantee compatibility,
interoperability, and continuity in the deployment and operational use of Intelligent Transport
Systems.
Despite this common purpose, significant differences persist among NAPs in terms of data availability,
the adoption of standards, and data quality. These inconsistencies can hinder the smooth exchange of
mobility data, limit interoperability and reusability for stakeholders operating across national borders
and ultimately decelerate seamless data exchange between NAPs and hence deter innovative crossborder ITS solutions. Addressing these challenges requires not only increasing data availability, but also
applying common metadata frameworks, and adopting harmonised standards, formats, and quality
requirements. Aligning road, traffic, travel and mobility data published on NAPs with the principles of
interoperability, compatibility, and continuity set out in the ITS Directive is therefore essential for
effective operational use, both at national level and across Europe.
The growing importance of these issues is further illustrated by recent regulatory developments.
Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/1926 amended by Delegated Regulation 2024/490
(MMTIS) [2], expands the scope of mandatory datasets to include, for example, parking availability and
tariffs, micromobility locations, as well as real -time information on travel times, and delays affecting
multimodal transport services. These new requirements further increase the responsibility for
Member States to enhance the availability, quality, and interoperability of their mobility data.
In practical terms, data providers/holders and data re-users established in one Member State can
effectively integrate and reuse multimodal mobility data from another Member State if such data is
made available via the NAP in an accurate, machine -readable manner, meets defined quality criteria,
and complies with co mmonly agreed EU specifications, standards, and is documented with
mobilityDCAT-AP as the preferred metadata specification. As an example, NAPCORE tackles the lack of
harmonisation of access nodes identifiers throughout datasets in the NAPs (Task 2.4), which can be
considered a barrier to seamless cross -border data integration. In parallel, Task 5.3 focuses on data

While this report does not focus exclusively on multimodal data availability, the multimodal use case
provides a concrete example of why interoperable data and metadata are critical, demonstrating how
harmonisation and quality form the foundation for the delivery of seamless ITS services and the
societal benefits derived from an integrated and interoperable European mobility ecosystem. NAPs
play a central role in this context by enab ling the discovery, access, exchange, and reuse of mobility
data across borders, thereby contributing to a more connected, efficient, and user -centric European
transport system.
Three different types of NAPs can be distinguished, with the following characteristics:
• Metadata directories: N APs that contain descriptions of the data and services essential for their
identification, assessment, and subscription, i.e., metadata. They provide facilities to authenticate
data providers and functionality to insert metadata records. Metadata directories offer search and
discovery services of the hosted records to end users and, through machine readable content, to
other upstream services . Metadata directories are not directly involved in the data exchange
between data providers and data consumers.
• Data platforms: N APs that contain descriptions of the data and services important for their
identification, assessment, and subscription by, i.e., metadata, together with the data and/or
services themselves. They provide facilities to authenticate data providers, and optional ly, data
consumers, and allow data and metadata records to be inserted into the NAP’s internal database.
Data platforms provide search and discovery services for hosted records to end users and, via
machine readable content, to other portals and distributi on interfaces. Data platforms are
intermediaries between data providers and data consumers, however, the data remains under the
ownership of the data providers.
• One can also identify the possibility of NAPs being of “hybrid” type, combining the functionalities
of both types, metadata directory and data platform, providing access to hosted data and services
while also enabling the discovery of datasets that remain outside the NAP infrastructure.
1.2. Importance of data and metadata availability and provision
Data discoverability and accessibility via a dedicated data portal is at the core of the success of
transportation related digital services, policy implementation and overall quality of a mobility data
ecosystem. Under the ITS Directive, NAPs are viewed as the point of access to mobility related data,
strategically aligned with the objectives of data discoverability, ease of access, availability, quality,
reliability, and interoperability. In their role of providing a d igital layer interlinking the different
mobility data ecosystems, guidance is found in legislation on which data should be made available.
ITS Directive’s article 6a ‘Availability of data and deployment of ITS services’, establishes that where
underlying information exists, data should be made available for the relevant geographical coverage
within a specified timeline and be accessible via N APs. Contrasting with the previous version, the
amended Directive makes these obligations mandatory and binding on EU Member States.
The connected Delegated Regulations , regulations and implementing acts list the categories , sub -
categories and data types to be made available for specific ITS domains, including multimodal travel
information services, real-time traffic information, road safety-related traffic information, and safe and
secure truck parking services. Together, these instruments determine a comprehensive framework for
mobility data availability and accessibility across Europe.

Other European regulatory initiatives, such as the Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Regulation (AFIR)
and the technical specification relating to the telematics subsystem of the rail system in the European
Union for interoperability of data sharing in rail transport (TEL -TSI), introduce further data-sharing
requirements within their respective domain.
The increasing number of sector-specific requirements demonstrates a broader transition in European
transport policy from data collection towards data -driven service provision. Data made available
through NAPs serves regulatory compliance and, more importantly, acts as a key enabler for digital
mobility services, network management, operational planning and evidence-based policy making.
The availability of standardised and machine -readable datasets supports the development of
multimodal journey planners, improves traffic information services, navigation applications, safety
monitoring tools and connected mobility solutions. So, ensuring that datasets are not only available
but also accessible and reusable has become a strategic objective of the European mobility data
panorama.
In addition to availability, recent legislation further highlights the importance of data quality,
timeliness and reliability. Across the ITS legal framework, data is expected to be of high quality for
operational use, requiring regular updates and the correction of identified inaccuracies whenever
possible. This highlights that the effectiveness of mobility services depends not only on the existence
of data but also on the trustworthiness of the information provided. In this context, NAPs additionally
take on the role of supporting transparency, consistency and quality assurance.

Effective discovery, access, and reuse of data depend on clear and standardised documentation of the
available datasets and services through relevant metadata. Inconsistent usage of metadata
frameworks across EU Member States limits cross -border reuse and may potentially reduce the
efficiency of EU-wide data sharing mechanisms.
As defined in the MMTIS DR and the EU Data Act (EU) 2023/2854 [3], metadata refers to structured
information describing the contents and use of data, facilitating its identification, discovery, and reuse.
When metadata is defined using common standards, profiles, and controlled vocabularies (e.g. DCAT-
AP), it enables bo th human and machine interpretation. Well -defined metadata improves computerbased data discoverability and helps users understand the content, structure, and connections
between datasets.
Beyond supporting discoverability, metadata constitutes a fundamental interoperability mechanism.
While datasets may be published using different systems, organisational structures or national
practices, metadata enables a common understanding of what info rmation is available and under
which conditions it can be accessed and reused. Metadata therefore facilitates communication
between data providers, NAP operators, service developers and end users by providing structured
information on dataset ownership, ac cessibility, licensing conditions, geographical coverage, update
frequency and applicable standards.
To support a seamless mobility data ecosystem, within the NAPCORE project, the mobility-specific
extension of DCAT -AP was developed. mobilityDCAT-AP defines standardised metadata elements for
mobility-related datasets, enabling consistent identification of aspects such as transport mode, data
theme and data formats. The proper adoption of mobilityDCAT-AP on NAPs helps to standardise the
description of mobility data and eases the exchange of this information.

The growing relevance of metadata is particularly evident in the context of emerging European data

approaches where datasets remain under the control of t heir original providers while being
discoverable through interconnected metadata catalogues and services. In such environments,

understood and accessed across organisational and national boundaries.
1.3. Objectives of this report
In line with this regulatory and operational background, the present deliverable focuses on the
assessment of data and metadata availability and provision across the NAPs using multiple
complementary approaches. These include voluntary reporting mechanisms, surveys and stakeholder
engagement through NAP showcases, and an analysis of the adoption of mobilityDCAT-AP.
This deliverable aims to support EU Member States in identifying gaps in data and metadata availability
for data categories covered by the relevant regulations, highlighting best practices and providing
actionable recommendations. By examining existing NAPs, the analysis assesses the completeness and
accessibility of available data and metadata, reviews current provision practices, and identifies
technical and organisational barriers to effective data sharing. In addition to that, the report highlights
replicable solutions through targeted NAP case studies. These illustrate different implementation
approaches and organisational solutions and support the exchange of experiences between Member
States. Overall, the outcomes of these activities aim to build a common understanding of current
practices and to identify areas where greater harmonisation and efficiency can be achieved, supporting
enhanced data insights and more effective reporting under the ITS Directive.
Attention is given to the role of metadata in supporting data discoverability, accessibility, transparency
and reuse. In this regard, the report examines aspects such as metadata standardisation, metadata
catalogue accessibility, metadata export capabilities and machine-readable interfaces. These elements
are increasingly relevant for enabling automated inventories, metadata harvesting mechanisms and
future metadata-assisted reporting processes.
The deliverable further aims to identify gaps in the availability of regulated data categories, highlight
challenges affecting the publication and management of mobility data, and explore opportunities for
improving data and metadata provision practices. In addition to the quantitative assessment, selected
NAP case studies provide an opportunity for exchange of experiences between Member States and
illustrate different implementation approaches, organisational solutions and practical experiences
related to NAP operation and development.
Ultimately, the report seeks to support Member States, Competent Authorities/National Bodies and
other stakeholders in better understanding the current state of the mobility data landscape, identifying
opportunities for improvement, and fostering greater harmonisation, interoperability and efficiency
across European NAPs. In doing so, it establishes a foundation for future work on metadata -assisted
reporting, automat ed metadata harvesting and the development of tools supporting reporting
obligations under the ITS Directive.

practices, this deliverable directly supports the objectives of NAPCORE Task 5.1, NAP Data Insights and

1.4. Limitations of the analysis
• Due to the reduced number of NAPs in focus, and the simplified assessment connected to the data
categories, results can be considered indicative rather than representative, and should be read as
an upper-bound, exploratory estimate of the state of data and metadata availability rather than a
definitive measurement.
• Binary indication of whether a dataset is available on the NAP loses in subtlety. For some specific
categories (e.g., available for e -scooters and cars), the real situation could be partial, potentially
under or overstating availability.
• Study indicates presence (for data availability) and not quality. Within NAPCORE, Task 5.3 Data
quality focus specifically on that aspect.
• Detailed analysis on the metadata catalogue is detailed but restricted to a single use case.
• Report presents a snapshot analysis.

2. Methodologies for assessing data and metadata availability and

Several methodologies for assessing data and metadata availability are accessible. However, no single

Therefore, Task 5.1 considered several approaches to assess metadata and data provision on NAPs.
The main ones are described in the next sub-sections.
2.1. Voluntary reporting mechanisms
Voluntary reporting mechanisms are a common approach used for assessing the availability and
provision of data and metadata across NAPs. Through questionnaires and self-assessment tasks, NAP
operators and other stakeholders can provide information regarding the availability of specific
datasets, metadata practices, standards adoption and the organisational processes behind them.
The advantage of this approach resides in its flexibility and relatively low implementation effort.
Questionnaires can be rapidly distributed, updated to reflect new regulatory requirements and
tailored to assess specific data domains, e.g., multimodal travel information services, traffic
information, alternative fuels infrastructure. Voluntary reporting enables the collection of contextual
information that is often not available through automated methods, including implementation
challenges, planned developments and organisational considerations.
However, voluntary reporting mechanisms also present significant limitations. The quality and
consistency of the results depend on the interpretation of questions and on the availability of accurate
information within the responding organisations. Furthermore, this approach does not always provide
a fully reliable assessment. Voluntary reporting should preferably be complemented by other
assessment approaches capable of providing additional validation and objective evidence.
Aware of these limits, Task 5.1 prepared a voluntary simplified “questionnaire” and distributed it
amongst Active Partners so that a current picture of the state of the NAPs, serving as a baseline and to
be complemented by additional mechanisms, could be refreshed.
Previous comparable approaches were embraced within the first phase of NAPCORE, and the results
showcased the fragilities of the approach, no matter how sophisticated the exercise or its presentation
was. It all came down to the availability of information and the reliability, completeness and
trustworthiness of the results.
2.2. Surveys and stakeholder engagement
While questionnaires provide structured information, stakeholder engagement activities offer
additional insights into the reasons behind the reported results. Interviews, workshops, NAP
showcases and direct exchanges with NAP operators enable a better understanding of the availability
of specific datasets, metadata provision, accessibility mechanisms, implementation approaches,
governance arrangements, technical challenges and organisational practices.
Stakeholder engagement activities are particularly valuable for identifying lessons learned, exchanging
experiences between Member States and highlighting practical solutions that may be replicated by
other NAPs. Such interactions also help clarify ambigui ties that may arise from questionnaire
responses and provide context for observed differences in data and metadata availability and
provision. They are especially useful for understanding metadata maturity (e.g., how complete, how
accurate, which standard was adopted) and how the accessibility mechanisms were implemented.

2.3. Automated inventories
Automated inventories for data availability assessment on the NAPs can be drastically simplified when
NAPs offer metadata catalogue exportation. This approach is scalable and objective, with its
effectiveness depending essentially on the existence of complete, consistent and machine -readable
metadata describing the datasets published by each NAP. When metadata catalogues are publicly
accessible an d follow common metadata standards, automated processes can be used to discover,
retrieve and analyse information about available datasets.
When metadata is available through mobilityDCAT -AP compliant endpoints, metadata harvesting
mechanisms and Resource Description Framework (RDF) query languages such as SPARQL can be used
to automatically generate and maintain national and cross -border inventories of available datasets.
Proper implementation of a common metadata model allows computer systems to quickly retrieve
dataset records across multiple Member States without manual intervention.
Furthermore, SPARQL queries can leverage metadata properties and controlled vocabularies defined
within mobilityDCAT-AP to automatically generate inventories focused on specific topics of interest.
For example, inventories can be restricted to datasets related to RTTI, MMTIS or AFIR, to specific
transport modes, or to datasets matching specific metadata attributes. This capability significantly
improves the efficiency of cross-border data discovery and enables targeted reporting and monitoring
activities without requiring manual filtering of dataset catalogues.
Compared with voluntary reporting approaches, surveys and stakeholder engagement, automated
inventories offer greater consistency, improved scalability and the ability to remain synchronised with
changes occurring in NAP metadata catalogues. These characteristics make automated inventories
particularly relevant for future metadata -assisted reporting activities under the ITS Directive and
constitute one of the strongest arguments for the widespread adoption of mobilityDCAT-AP, metadata
exports and machine -readable metadata interfaces across European NAPs. Moreover, they can take
place at any point in time and can serve as a mechanism for NAP operators to obtain an instant status
of the metadata catalogue offered, exposing gaps in data provision.
This approach nevertheless depends on the proper, complete adoption of mobilityDCAT -AP, on the
metadata accurately reflecting the content of the corresponding data, and on the NAP permitting the
harvesting of the metadata catalogue. Once these conditions are met, automated inventories perform
well; achieving them, however, requires extra preparatory steps from NAP operators.

3. Metadata in the NAPs
Metadata is central to the NAPs operations and a key enabler of data reuse. In this section we explore
the main advantages from the perspective of the different stakeholders involved, when metadata is
properly integrated as a building block of the NAP oper ations, the principles involved in metadata
submission, and Application Programming Interfaces (API) as machine-to-machine communication and
automated data exchange enablers.
3.1. Standardised metadata
Standardisation of metadata is a pre -requirement for interoperability, particularly when systems are
required to communicate, aggregate, integrate, exchange and/or process information without
ambiguity. Metadata reaches its full value when it is standardis ed, and in this case the foundation is
provided by semantic technologies, such as controlled vocabularies with unique resource identifiers.
W3C’s Data Catalogue Vocabulary (DCAT) is an RDF vocabulary designed to facilitate interoperability
between data catalogues published on the Web. It enables publishers to describe datasets and data
services using a standard model and vocabulary, supporting the consistent consumption and
aggregation o f metadata from multiple catalogues and improving overall discoverability. DCAT also
supports decentralised catalogue publishing and enables federated search across distributed
catalogues through a shared query structure. Aggregated DCAT metadata can addit ionally serve as a
manifest for digital preservation [9].
The SEMIC Support Center defines an Application Profile (AP) as a data specification aimed to facilitate
the data exchange in a well -defined application context. An AP re -uses concepts from one or more

3.3. Principles for metadata submission to the NAP
With all the advantages of metadata mentioned, it is important to also establish roles, responsibilities
and specifications so that the integration in the NAPs is streamlined and facilitated.
The obligation to submit metadata associated to the datasets comes from multiple Delegated
Regulations adopted under the ITS Directive, e.g., in the Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/1926
(MMTIS), Article 3 National Access Points states: ‘Member States shall reach an agreement, in
cooperation with relevant ITS stakeholders, on the metadata requirements. The data
providers/holders shall ensure that they provide the metadata based on those requirements.’ , or in
Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/670 (RTTI), Article 3 that reads: ‘Public and private data
providers/holders shall ensure that they provide the metadata in order to allow data re-users to
discover and use datasets via national access points.’
Member States are therefore responsible for establishing metadata requirements such as:
• Metadata standard to be used, in this case mobilityDCAT-AP;

• Metadata elements and their prescribed obligation level (e.g., mandatory, recommended,
optional);
• The use of specific controlled vocabularies;
• Metadata quality (completeness, links check, validated…);
• Metadata submission method (manually, via forms, structured digital entry, via API…).
Data providers/holders are primarily responsible for providing metadata and ensuring that it is done
in the most complete, consistent, accurate manner.
Regardless of the submission method used, the metadata provided should satisfy a small set of
principles:
• it should be complete, covering at least the elements made mandatory by the national
requirements;
• accurate, describing the dataset as it is published rather than as it was planned; consistent, using
the prescribed controlled vocabularies so that records from different data providers/holders
remain comparable;
• up-to-date, revised whenever the dataset, its distributions or its access conditions change;
• machine-readable, so that the record can be retrieved, validated and reused without manual
intervention. Identifiers deserve particular attention, since persistent and unique identifiers for
datasets, distributions and organisations are what allow records to be linked, deduplicated and
harvested across NAPs.
Metadata can reach the NAP in several ways. The methods described below are not mutually exclusive
and can be in practice combined within the same NAP, depending on the size and the technical
maturity of each data holder/provider.
Manual metadata entry, in which the data holder types the record directly into the NAP interface, is
the most flexible method and the one with the lowest entry barrier for occasional contributors. It is
also the most error-prone and the most expensive to maintain, as values are re-typed for every dataset,
free-text entries drift away from the agreed vocabularies, and updates depend entirely on the data
holder remembering to make them. It remains appropriate for small metadata catalogues and for
organisations that publish only a few datasets.
Structured entry (e.g., digital forms) constrains what can be submitted. Forms that expose the
mandatory, recommended and optional elements defined in the national requirements, with selection
lists bound to the prescribed controlled vocabularies, remove most of the ambiguity of free-text entry
and allow a record to be validated at the moment it is created. This offers a good balance between the
effort asked of data providers/holders and the consistency of the resulting metadata catalogue.
Automated extraction, in which the NAP derives metadata directly from the dataset or from the service
endpoint, often feasible for part of a record but not for all of it. Technical and structural elements, such
as the format and encoding, the standard or profile applied, the spatial and temporal coverage, the
observed update frequency and the availability of the distribution itself, can often be derived from the
resource, and structured exchange standards such as DATEX II, TN-ITS, NeTEx and SIRI carry enough
information for this to be done reliably. Descriptive, legal and organisational elements, such as the
purpose of the dataset, the responsible organisation and its contact point, licensing and access
conditions, and any declared quali ty, cannot be inferred from the data and still must be supplied by
the data holder. Automated extraction is therefore best understood as a means of pre -filling a record
and of continuously checking that it still matches the published data, rather than as a replacement for
submission.

Metadata harvesting moves a record between metadata catalogues instead of creating it. Where a
data holder, or a regional or sectoral portal, already maintains a metadata catalogue that follows the
same application profile, the NAP can collect those records and keep them synchronised, so that the
metadata is maintained once and published in several places.
The same mechanism operating between NAPs is what would allow a cross -border data holder to
register a dataset only once, as noted among the advantages for data providers earlier in this chapter.
Harvesting assumes that the source metadata catalogue is publicly accessible, exposes a machine -
readable serialisation and applies the same profile (or that a mapping is provided); where these
conditions are met, it is the least burdensome method for data providers/holders and the most
sustainable one for NAP operators.
Metadata quality and governance do not follow automatically from the submission method and need
to be organised explicitly.
Validation is the first control point. A submitted record can be checked automatically against the
application profile, verifying whether the mandatory elements are present, whether values come from
the prescribed vocabularies, whether identifiers are well formed and whether the declared links
resolve, with the result returned to the data holder before publication. Correcting a record at that
moment is considerably cheaper than correcting a metadata catalogue afterwards.
Responsibilities should be explicit. The data holder submits the record and keeps it current; the NAP
operator can validate it , publishes it and monitors that it remains resolvable; and the competent
authority verifies that the resulting metadata catalogue meets the requirements set out in the ITS
Directive and its Delegated Regulations.
To note that not all NAPs perform validation before publishing it. Alternatively, it can be published
after a syntax check . The validation can then occur at a later stage by the NAP operator and /or the
Competent Authorities/National Bodies. Quality information is in practice self -declared by the data
holder/provider, so when it is published it should remain open to verification rather than just accepted
at submission point.
Finally, metadata has a lifecycle. Records should be revised when the dataset, its distributions or its
access conditions change, and datasets that are withdrawn should be marked as discontinued rather
than silently removed, so that users and harvesting sy stems can tell a discontinued dataset from a
temporary failure. Recording the date of the last modification, and the version of the application
profile against which a record was created, makes both the maintenance effort and the age of the
metadata catalogue visible.

3.4. Role of standardised APIs in metadata provision and retrieval
A metadata API is the interface at which two distinct roles meet and separating them clarifies what is
expected of whom:
• Provision is what NAP does: it exposes its metadata catalogue at a stable, machine -readable
endpoint and keeps it current, latest version.
• Retrieval is what a consumer (re-user, an application or another NAP) does: it reads that endpoint
in a consistent and automated manner, to obtain the whole catalogue or a selected part of it.
Provision is a responsibility of the NAP operator, whereas retrieval is a capability offered to consumers.
Where both work, discoverability improves markedly, as users, search engines and third -party tools
can find the full data offer and query it for specific datasets instead of browsing a NAP manually.
What is exchanged and how it is exchanged are two separate layers, and they must not be confused.
mobilityDCAT-AP is not an API; it is an application profile, which specifies which elements a metadata
record carries, how they are named and which controlled vocabularies their values are drawn from.
The API is the mechanism that delivers records described in that pr ofile. Both layers need to be
standardised for automated reuse to work. If an endpoint provides records in a country -specific or ad
hoc format, every consumer still needs to create its own mapping. Likewise, a record that fully complies
with mobilityDCAT -AP has limited value if it can only be accessed through a web page rather than a
machine-readable endpoint.
This is why NAPs should adopt a standardised metadata specification from the outset, explicitly
mobilityDCAT-AP.

On the provision side, the simplest form is a full export: the complete metadata catalogue serialised in
a machine -readable format and published at a stable URL address, e.g., as the Belgian NAP does ,
accessible from https://transportdata.be/catalog.rdf. An export of this kind is not a demanding
capability for a NAP operator to offer and keep running, and it is sufficient for harvesting and for any
consumer prepared to process the whole catalogue. It is not, however, a query interface: selecting a
subset, such as the datasets of one cat egory, one region or one data holder, or only those changed
since a given date, is left to the consumer, who has to retrieve everything first and filter it afterwards,
for instance with a local SPARQL query.
A query API moves that selection from the consumer to the server, which then returns what was asked
for. The two are complementary rather than alternatives, the export serving harvesters and bulk reuse,
the query API serving interactive discovery and lightweight clients.
A full export need not arrive in a single response. Interestingly, the Belgian metadata catalogue is
paged; one request returns 100 of its 192 datasets, and the rest is reached only by following a link to
the next page. The paging is declared inside the returned file, as a few statements in the Hydra
vocabulary giving the total number of items, the number of items per page and the address of the next
page. These statements are not part of mobilityDCAT -AP, and nothing in the catalogue record points
to them, so a consumer that starts from the catalogue and follows its dataset links reaches the end of
the first page without any error or warning, having collected barely half of what the NAP publishes.
This is the distinction between the profile and the mechanism again, seen from the retrieval side ; the
profile prescribes what a record contains, but whether a consumer has obtained the whole metadata
catalogue is settled by the retrieval contract. Where an export is paged, that should be stated in the
documentation of the endpoint, and a harvester should be built to follow the paging rather than to
assume a single complete file.
Where both layers are in place, metadata can be retrieved and processed automatically, without
laborious manual work. This is what makes accurate data catalogues, connection to other national or
EU-level platforms and the integration into mobility services possible in practice. It is also the
precondition for the metadata harvesting described above and it works best where the source NAP
provides a machine-readable endpoint and both sides apply the same application profile.
Therefore, a major conclusion that can be derived from this section is that metadata when accessible
can be key to the assessment of data availability in the NAPs, allowing to go beyond a basic assessment
of mere presence, enabling also to check the compliance of the offered content in for example formats
used, or update frequency.

5. Metadata based assessment
5.1. Automated versus manual reporting
The preceding chapters assess data and metadata availability from what NAP operators report about
themselves. Chapter 2 describes three ways of collecting that report: voluntary reporting mechanisms,
surveys and stakeholder engagement, and automated invent ories. The first two depend on a person
answering a form. The third reads the metadata catalogue that the NAP already publishes. This chapter
examines what changes when the third is used in place of the first two.
The two approaches differ in the kind of statement they produce. A questionnaire records claims. For
each of the 171 data categories drawn from the annexes of the Delegated Regulations the operator
states Yes or No. That set is fixed and identical for every NAP questionnaire. A metadata catalogue
records evidence instead, a body of dataset descriptions, each of which can be followed to a URL and
checked.
The two approaches are specific. A questionnaire answer cannot be verified, and it may record intent,
or (operators) memory, rather than what is published. A metadata catalogue cannot be wrong about
what it contains, but a category that is genuinely available and not described in it stays invisible to an
automated reading. How much of the manual report the automated one can carry therefore depends
on how completely the catalogue is attributed and how far the controlled vocabulary reaches, and
both of those are improving.
5.2. mobilityDCAT-AP based automated metadata assessment
An automated assessment reads a single input, the NAP’s metadata catalogue, serialised according to
mobilityDCAT-AP and retrieved from a published endpoint. Retrieval is not always a single request.
A catalogue may be paged, in which case the paging is declared inside the returned file and must be
followed, as Section 3.4 describes. A consumer that stops at the first response only partially assesses
the catalogue.
The assessment then must bridge two vocabularies. The 171 data categories are phrased in the
language of the Delegated Regulation annexes, while the metadata catalogue describes datasets using
the mobilityDCAT-AP data content category vocabulary.
The comparison here uses version 1.1.1, the Mobility Theme concept scheme, which defines 19 data
content categories and 102 sub -categories. Establishing a category from the catalogue therefore
requires a vocabulary term of matching meaning. That comparison is a property of the sp ecification
rather than of any individual NAP, and it is made once against the vocabulary as published. To note,
that NAPCORE’s task 4.4 is now enlarging the controlled vocabularies to better represent the data
categories listed in regulations. This improvement will be key for quality of data availability results.
Each category then falls into one of three states. There is an exact match with a metadata dataset,
meaning the vocabulary has a term for the category and a dataset in the metadata catalogue carries
it. There is a match in the vocabulary that no metadata dataset uses. Or there is no match in the

For the Belgian case the metadata catalogue in mobilityDCAT-AP format replaces the metadata part of
the questionnaire outright and about a fifth of the data availability part . It also adds evidence on
publishers, standards, protocols, update frequencies and coverage that the questionnaire does not
collect. The remaining gap is not a permanent property of the method. It has two causes and both are

closing. Today 51 % of the categories have no match in the vocabulary, which successive releases of

8. Conclusions
This section brings together the key findings and recommendations set out in the preceding sections.
Section 8.1 summarises the key findings of the assessment, together with the limitations that should
be kept in mind when interpreting them. Section 8.2 outlines the next steps planned to extend and
consolidate this work.
Across all surveyed NAPs, several recurring success factors emerged:
• Strong cooperation with national authorities and data providers.
• Comprehensive documentation, schemas, and examples for datasets.
• Integration with national open-data infrastructures to reduce duplication of effort.
• User-friendly discovery tools, such as interactive maps and search functionalities.
• Adoption of mobilityDCAT-AP.
• Digital metadata submission workflows that simplify participation by data providers.
• Provision of metadata APIs to support automated access and interoperability.
• Public metadata catalogues that improve dataset discoverability.
• Support mechanisms for smaller organisations that may lack technical capabilities.
• Continuous improvement programmes and future-proofing efforts.
8.1. Summary of key findings
The present assessment evaluates only the availability of datasets associated with the categories
defined in the regulations connected to the ITS Directive . Since compliance with additional
requirements concerning standards, metadata, accessibility, geographical coverage, interoperability
and data quality was not assessed, the results should not be interpreted as compliance levels. Inclusion
of these requireme nts would inevitably reduce, or at best maintain, the number of categories
considered fully compliant. Therefore, we can regard the results as an upper-bound estimate.
The least -available categories across all five regulations point to challenges related to stakeholder
integration, governance fragmentation, operational information, commercial and pricing information,
digitalisation maturity, derived information, service maturity, infrastructure maturity, none mapped
into the low-implementation-effort zone.
Availability of metadata alone does not always imply that the underlying data itself is described in
sufficient detail. Some records point only to a URL and general description rather than to a fully
specified distribution. Metadata information should be prioritised, as well as its correctness and
completeness.
Every NAP in the sample publishes metadata and completeness is 82% on average, but on average only
41% make it retrievable. This should be prioritised.
Although the automated inventory based on metadata did not retrieve ideal results, it remains the
most suitable basis for a complete assessment for several reasons:

This report establishes a useful overview of the panorama of NAPs (through the selected sample) that
draws attention to future challenges connected to the main objective of Task 5.1.
The more detailed study on metadata practices is specifically valuable as metadata can be seen as a

streamlining automated reporting.
