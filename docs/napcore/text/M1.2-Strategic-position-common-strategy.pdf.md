Overview of this milestone and document
This document represents M1.2 of WG1 and proposes a strategic position and common strategy for
cooperation with major stakeholders in the mobility landscape, especially those not connected in any
formal way to NAPCORE activities – i.e. project partners or members of the Advisory Board. The
strategy is defined by identifying specific goals and f or each goal, strategic actions for NAPCORE
partnership are defined to reach the respective goal. The document also gives a comprehensive
overview of all the stakeholders that are relevant for NAPCORE, acros s different transport modes.
The document is divided in six chapters and a set of Annexes . The first chapter is the introduction,
followed by four main chapters and the conclusions in chapter six. Annex 1 contains a detailed
description of the members of NAPCORE Adv isory Board. Annex 2 documents a selection of relevant
national standardisation activities identified by NAPCORE WG4. Finally, the categorization of relevant
stakeholders within WG5 as of September 2023 is presented in Annex 3.
The introduction provides an overview of the mobility market in terms of actors and transport services,
as well as a review of other results from NAPCORE relevant for this milestone.
Chapter Two deals with several topics: overview of NAPCORE Advisory Board; establishing major
external stakeholders’ categories targeted for cooperation; presentation of a comprehensive list of
potential major external stakeholders .
Chapter Three is the main chapter, and it describes in detail the proposed goals for cooperation and
strategic actions that define the strategic position and common strategy for cooperation with major
stakeholders, those not represented in NAPCORE nor the Advisory Board.
Chapter Four presents potential cooperation activities with other initia tives such as X4ITS, DFRS or C-
Roads platform.
Finally, Chapter Five briefly describes potential impacts of emerging technologies (e.g. Internet of
Things, Block chain, Artificial Intelligence) on NAP operations.

1. Introduction
This document was developed as part of Task 1.2 activities in Working Group 1 of the NAPCORE project.
The main objective of Task 1.2 is to monitor existing and upcoming projects, activities or initiatives in
the mobility data and data exchange domain . Relevant valuable results from these activities will be
considered or incorporated via regular liaisons.
This document represents M1.2 of WG1 and focuses on identifying specific goals for cooperation with
major stakeholders in the mobility landscape , especially those not connected in any formal way t o
NAPCORE activities – i.e. NAPCORE partners o r members of the Advisory Board. The document also
gives a comprehensive overview of all the stakeholders that are relevant for NAPCORE, across different
transport modes.
Task 1.2 started the work in March 2023 and since then regular meetings were he ld to define a
common methodology and content for this report . The first challenge was how to define a common
strategy that considers both the ongoing activities of NAPCORE, and the potential needs or
requirements of stakeholders involved in the NAP ecosystem. After ma ny discussions between Task
1.2 members, it was agreed that a pragmatic approach is needed , and t hat the targeted stakeholders
should be categorized in a small number of generic categories.
As a result, the strategic position and common strategy we propose consists in several goals for
cooperation with external stakeholders . The goals are presented in Chapter Three and for each one,
strategic actions for NAPCORE partnership are defined to reach the respective goal.
The goals for cooperation are the result of the expert knowledge of the partners involved in Task 1.2 .
These goals do not represent any obligations for NAPCORE partners , instead they are meant as a
guiding framework to facilitate and focus cooperation activities with external stakeholders.
The goals for cooperation and the strategic actions were developed with the primary objective of
contributing to NAPCORE ’s mission: promote NAPs and support their harmonised implementation at
European level. In this sense, NAPCORE should cooperate with external stakeholders to understand
their needs for data exchange through the NAPs.
1.1. The mobility market
This section deals with the mobility of persons, and does not consider the transport of goods
(commercial vehicles). The two travel domains may overlap when mobility data are at stake (e.g. for
transport by rail).
1.1.1. What is mobility
Mobility is a decision of a person – the traveller – to move/perform a trip from a geographical Origin
to a geographical Destination D (OD trip) – in a given territory ( mobility area) - for a given reason ( trip
purpose) at a given date/time ( trip time). Mobility is achieved either only by on-foot travel – walking –
usually for very short distance, or through the individual use of a transport mean (actives modes or
private cars and other motorized modes), or through the use of one (or a set of) transport modes shared
with other travellers, or through a combination of these options .
To assist a traveller moving, the “ mobility market ” proposes “mobility services” before, during and
after the travel1.

Part of the mobility services are digital information services and therefore focusing on mobility data
(see mobility datasets below).
As regards passenger mobility, th e car remains the dominant 2 form of inland transport in all EU
Member States (MSs). Private cars and motorised two - (or three -) wheels and potentially some
categories of car-pooling are the only modes which can serve with one vehicle any mobility needs over
the European territory from any geographical origin to any geographical destinatio n (end-to-end OD
trip), except in cases where car usage or car parking is restricted in specific urban areas.
Restricting car usage is more and more common in major conurbations and is also one of the
instruments promoted by the Clean Air Directive. This is where UVAR’s stem from.
Where car usage is limited and/or restricted, collective public transport and active mobility (by bike or
by foot) are usually highly developed and attractive.
1.1.2. Mobility alternatives for passenger transport depend on the location of the origin and
destination of the travel
The other private modes allowing to serve on their own any end -to-end OD trip are active modes
(walking and (e -)bike or (e -)scooter)3, but of course this is only possible for trips located in one single
urban area, and when O and D are close to each other.
The “urban area” is not often an administrative area, but rather a functional area with permeable limits
over which the daily-life mobility needs of local citizens can be served. The local citizen can find in the
urban area the various services provided by a city and its hinterland (which might include some rural
areas). These services are purposes of trips: home, education, health, work, shopping, administration,
culture, religion, entertainment...
By far, most of mobility trips are confined in one single urban area . When the local travellers do not
use private car or private active modes, they use to travel between the physical origin and the physical
destination of their trip within the single urban area:
• either one public transport mode only (like taxi, or Uber-type services) or
• a combination of transport modes including always some walking to access a stop point of a
public transport service at the origin of the trip or to egress from a stop point to the final
destination of the trip.
In some cases the access to the public transport stop point can be done with a private mean
(car, motorised two -wheels or (e-)bike or (e-)scooter).
In all cases, there is also one public transport mode which can be available for free at the very
beginning or destination of the trip: the lift , which is the second most used mode in local mobility
in terms of number of passengers (but of course not in t erms of passenger x km).
The public transport modes which can be used between two public transport stop points within the
same urban area are providing:
• collective public transport scheduled or on -demand services, by bus (various categories with
more or less capacity and access restriction to specific categories of users, including touristic
coach or bus services), by tram or Light Rail, by (urban) metro or (regional) metro -like tra in

The public transport modes which can be used for trips between two different urban areas depend on

rules which apply for the services between the two urban areas (or “urban nodes”) are set up by a mix
of authorities which can be national, European or even international, depending if the two urban areas
are in the same country, or in two different EU countries, or if one urban area is outside of the
European Union territory (in Europe at large – 47 countries, or outside Europe).

1.1.5. Interoperability requirements
Road traffic and some public transport services by road, by air, by rail or waterborne and their relevant
assets (infrastructure, vehicles, equipment) require some kind of “interoperability” at (national or)
European level - to allow the movement of vehicles and people throughout (a country or) Europe 10,
when the origin and destination of the trips are located in different cities or countries. Other
modes/services stay purely local and do not require (national or European) interoperability, even if
they are fully “multimodal” like local public transport modes/services (including ticketing) already
implemented in most important urban areas. This is why in the latter case the decisions regarding
technical specifications for public transport are usually in the hands of competent national and/or local
authorities (a challenge being to integrate properly locally “alternative modes” as “complementary
services” to collective public transport).
The type of data needed to support public authorities and to be delivered to the NAPs are related to
the various modes used along the travel chain between the origin and the destination: data describing
the transport supply (assets and services); data desc ribing the customers potential needs; data flows
on each “mode”, resulting from counting or ticket sells for the modal journey, data on OD trips and

1.1.6. Mobility data sets and stakeholders
Mobility datasets are related not only to the travel option including effective, reliable and secure
transportation, but also to infrastructure, vehicles, and I ntelligent Transport Systems components
involved in the transport chain.
The infrastructure may vary in nature and be shared - or not - among various types of vehicles. The
vehicle might be a private vehicle (owned by an individual for passenger travel or by a company for
goods travel) or a public transport vehicle either used for a private use (like Uber for passenger travel
or a road haulage operator for goods) or for a collective use for passenger transport (like a bus)).
Therefore, in addition to the customers and transport service providers, the mobility stakeholders are
those involved in:
• the provision of infrastructure and vehicles: public authorities, civil works companies, vehicle
and equipment manufacturers…
• the use of infrastructure and vehicles: public authorities, private or public operators, vehicle
service providers, individuals… The use of vehicle can address the vehicle itself (technical
requirements), the right to use the vehicle (driver license), the right to offer a transport service
(registered companies and drivers).
• the provision of information on transport services (e.g. transport offers, journey planning,
transport services booking and purchase): public authorities, private or public operators,
information service providers.
The “ digital mobility services ” are covering “ on-line” and/or “ off-line” information on transport
services for “connected” travellers.
Mobility data targeted by NAPCORE encompass static and dynamic time datasets (in the form of data
register and/or database and/or data warehouse and/or data marketplace managed in metadata
directory or data platform) which shall be used for identifying, monitoring, understanding, organisin g,
regulating, and facilitating the movements of people, freight, and vehicles. Some services are limited
to the provision of digital information on the transport services but may include the booking and the
purchase of tickets through internet when a comm ercial transaction is required.
It can be noticed that the MMTIS regulation is entitled “MultiModal Travel Information Services” but
deals with transport services of people and vehicles, while “mobility services” may also include “travel
services” which are not actual transport services but which complement them and depend on the
[page 12]
12
purpose11 of the trip at either end of the OD trip 12 or at intermediate stops 13 of the travel chain. These
travel services might be referred to as “location -based” services, since they are provided to customers
before or after using a vehicle (including at transfer points between two vehicles).
For passenger travel, there exist various types of travel service providers, and the type of transport
service is determined by two primary categories:
• “commercial services” provided on request for a price paid by the consumer, at a rate which
allows the Travel Service Provider to self -sustain the delivery of the service;
• “PSO services ” (PSO=Public Service Obligations) which are transport services not fully
commercially viable, and are provided with the support of competent public authorities and
taxpayers contributions, which means services operated under “public service contracts”
agreed between the operator and competent “public transport authorities” (“PTAs”) . The
contract includes public service obligations to be applied by the operator, and the price to be
paid by the customer (if any) is decided by the PTA.
The largest part of local public transport service, and all urban rail services are provided under PSO,
which means that the PTAs are a stakeholder influencing both the transport supply and the transport
demand including in terms of data provision, data co llection and data management.
Passenger “commercial” travel services are operated by private companies such as:
• collective transport services operators (e.g. airlines, mainline rail companies, coach companies
like Flixbus, Blablacar Bus, Alsa, Marino bus) or
• taxis or other services with a driver (e.g. Uber, Bolt) or without driver (e.g. bike -sharing,
scooter-sharing) or
• car rental companies or carsharing companies etc. or
• travel agencies, mainly organisers and most often not operators or
• digital mobility services providers (MaaS).
Although most of the transport services providers can be regarded as public transport operators, the
term PTO is usually applied to public transport operators providing collective transport services
operated under public services obligations or PTOs which are “in -house” operators owned by the
PTA.
In urban areas the services provided by the PTOs are part of an integrated passenger public transport
system and are integrated in a fare policy system combining bus lines (and sometimes on -demand bus
or shared taxi services) and in bigger conurbations tra m/light rail lines, metro lines, and suburban rail
lines (and sometimes ferry services like in Lisbon or waterborne services like the Vaporetto in Venice).
The integration is a political decision of competent public authorities, which are more and more
responsible for the overall sustainable mobility policy at the urban area level.

1.2.1. Results from T5.4
Milestone report M5.6 presents the status of the WG5 Task 5.4 “Accelerate the provision of data on
the NAPs” and elaborates on initial reflections on strategies and actions to motivate private,
international organisations to provide data on the NAPs. This work is crucial, as cooperation with
various stakeholders and organisations is required in order to implement the Delegated Regulations,
especially MMTIS and the new RTTI. Due to the high number of stakeholders, also because of the
extension of the require ments to the entire European mobility network, motivating stakeholders to
provide data on the NAPs proves to be challenging. To address these challenges, different kind of
measures are required, e.g. organisational, communicational and strategic ones.
More specifically, the milestone report has the following objectives:
1. Identification and categorisation of relevant organisations ( see Annex 3)
2. Reflection on strategies and actions to address and motive these organisations to provide data
on the NAPs
The identified stakeholders have been grouped according to their geographical scope (national,
international), type (data holder, service provider) and nature (public, private). In further consequence,
a short - and long -term vision for national as well as international stakeholders was developed ( see
Annex 3 ). The measures include communication guidelines, Q&As, best practices, technical support
and actions to raise awareness.
The outcome of the process illustrated that a joint approach regarding the stakeholders’ motivation to
provide data on the NAPs is necessary to maximise synergies but also to reduce the risk of multiple
contacts and (potential) contradictory messages being exchanged. Furthermore, the establishment of
a trustworthy rapport between NAPCORE and organisations via signed contracts and agreements
paves the way for more comprehensive collaboration. To tackle challenges regarding Compliance
Assessment it is crucial to define a framework of harmonised strategies and processes. The creation of
an incentive system or a selection of sanctioning options in case of non -compliance is to be defined.
Future attention within the NAPCORE project should revolve around the follo wing activities:
• Increase the understanding and awareness of the benefits and importance of the ITS Directive
and the role of private international organisations in achieving its objectives
• Foster the dialogue with and better understanding of relevant stakeholders
• Streamline the National Bodies for the NAPs
• Evaluation incentives for stakeholders to provide data on the NAPs
In the first of half of 2024, first actions have been taken to raise awareness of the ITS Directive among
relevant stakeholders and to foster the dialogue with them. In order to better understand the
concerns, experiences and challenges stakeholders face w hen sharing data on the NAPs, a survey has
been conducted and an interactive webinar was organised.

1.2.3. Results from WG2
In order to achieve more interoperability, it is imperative to analyse the status of NAP implementation
in all MSs with respect to data standards, reference profiles and metadata used and identify eventual
gaps. This work is carried out as part of Task 2.2 - and consolidated into the Milestone 2.7 report. This
report is an updated version of NAPCORE Milestone 2.4, which was limited to the analysis of data
standards. In addition to updating such analysis, M2.7 extends it by including the identification of ga ps
and listing recommendations and requirements for profiles and metadata. In that sense, the report
provides an analysis, inventory of shortcomings and recommendation of the three subjects that
constitute the referred task, as well as a set of recommendat ions and requirements destined to
standardisation bodies (main stakeholders).
The data standards mentioned in the DRs and NAPCORE WG4 (Data Exchange Standards) are used for
the analysis in this report. Open data standards such as GTFS, rail, and air data models are also
included. It is worth mentioning that some of these specificati ons, such as GTFS (and GTFS -RT), OJP,
and IATA, have been included in the analysis since they are frequently used in the mobility domain;
however, they are not referred to in the EU Delegated Regulations. The following standards are part
of the analysis: I NSPIRE, DATEX II, TN -ITS, Transmodel, NeTEx, SIRI, GTFS & GTFS -RT, OJP, IATA, TAP -
TSI, and GBFS.
The adopted methodological approach for this report is based on a combination of targeted desktop
research and extensive analysis of data collected by the other WGs of NAPCORE, later supplemented
by internal WG discussions and expert review by some project partners and external stakeholders from
standardisation entities (e.g., MobilityData, EU Agency from Railways).
Section 2 details the analysis of data standards. As part of this analysis, targeted research was a major
task carried out to identify gaps in the standards. Such research allowed one to verify which standards
can cover (totally or partially) the elements addressed by each data category specified at the four DRs.
Recommendations for further development of data standards followed the gap identification process.
Moreover, by using outputs of other WGs and extending them with an internal (Task 2.2) survey and
review procedure, we were able to analyse the use of standards in the NAPs, quantifying the coverage
of each data category, as well as the standards (or formats) used for such coverage.
Additionally, the concept of profiles is available to support the different types of services using these
standards. A profile is a subset of the entire standard, only containing the relevant data elements
required for a specific use case. At first, a revi ew of existing European and national profiles was
conducted by consulting databases from the standardisation entities and completed with inputs from
Task 2.2 members. Based on this initial research, gaps in the scenario of the existing profiles that may
hinder interoperability were identified, such as missing profiles for specific use cases and governance
issues for national profiles, among others. Then, a set of recommendations was drafted in order to
tackle those gaps.
In addition to standards and profiles, detailed and standardised descriptions of datasets, i.e. metadata,
are also crucial for enabling the discoverability of mobility data, which is one of the main objectives
behind the existence of the NAPs. Outcomes of sWG4.4 are used to describe and detail relevant
aspects concerning current recommended metadata specifications for the NAPs, as well as to provide
a list of requirements concerning metadata. Furthermore, results of the NAP Levels of Service (LoS)
self-assessment carried out under Task 2.1 were used to assess how the NAPs currently align/adhere
to these requirements and specifications.

Central European cross -border cooperation for ITS, short “X4ITS” is a CEF funded deployment project,

road administrations and traffic information service providers. Additionally to previous corridor
projects, X4ITS not only focuses on high -level network, but also on secondary road network and multi -
modal or urban nodes.
The X4ITS corridor is located in the East and Central European (CE) area and combines six different
countries with five core TEN -T network corridors, including Austria, Croatia, Czechia, Hungary,
Romania and Slovenia.
In connection to NAPCORE, part of X4ITS is an enlarged cooperation with NAPCORE and a harmonised
integrated (C -)ITS implementation, this takes place especially in work packages 1,2 and 4. Work
package 1 includes not only project management and communication, but also enhanced cross -
corridor cooperation.
In X4ITS Task 1.3 there will be on the one hand an interconnection with other corridor projects like
MERIDIAN and MATIS, on the other hand with NAPCORE and the European C -Roads Platform.
Cooperation with NAPCORE will support the implementation of harmonised recomme ndations for
upgrading NAPs. Therefore, X4ITS will participate in discussions and coordination activities to engage
in a harmonised integrated implementation of ITS and C -ITS in Europe. Focus will be the end -user
support and an increase of accessi bility of mobility data. There will be a participation in workshops and
events, starting with a workshop at the NAPCORE Mobility Data Days in Budapest. The workshop links
not only NAPCORE and X4ITS but aims to connect the other corridor projects MERIDIAN and MATIS as
well. In this session, expectations and further needs will be discussed.
X4ITS Work package 4 “Data access and collection” will result in an improvement and harmonisation
of data exchange standards. The implementation of DATEX II nodes will take place in X4ITS Task 4.1,
considering recommendations worked out in NAPCORE. X4ITS features the implementation of DATEX

Hungary and Slovenia will be upgraded, Croatia will di gitise video surveillance as well as detection

4.2. DFRS
Significantly improving road safety across Europe for all road users requires the mass involvement of
vehicle manufacturers, traffic information service providers, road operators, authorities and agencies,
and policy makers/public authorities. Such a level of participation is necessary to ensure the pace and
critical mass of safety data required for comprehensive safety related traffic information services.
In various European MSs, the number of seriously injured victims of road crashes continues to increase,
whereas the decrease in the number of road deaths is stagnating. Governments are actively working
together to structurally improve road safety. Data sharing of safety related e vents can play an
important role in this. Warning road users about dangerous situations, such as temporary slippery
road, reduced visibility, broken -down vehicles, and accidents, can prevent accidents and limit
consequences. The European regulatory framewo rk thereto is embedded in the European Commission
DR 886/2013 of 15 May 2013.
The Data For Road Safety (DFRS) Safety Related Traffic Information (SRTI) Ecosystem has been
established by MSs/public authorities and the industry to provide a multi -party contractual framework
for the exchange of safety related traffic information among the participants, and to make the SRTI
events mentioned in the DR 886/201 3 available through the NAPs.
NAPs as central platforms for mobility data facilitate access, easy exchange, and reuse of transport
related data in Europe, in order to help support the provision of EU -wide interoperable travel and
traffic services to end users. Safety related data event s are an important part of the intelligence that
can be made available through the NAPs.
DFRS and NAPCORE agreed to:
• Exchange intelligence on each other’s governance set -up and working of the ecosystem

• The creation of a win-win situation for both parties by enabling the pooling of knowledge through
the respective stakeholder communities
• Exchange on best practices in terms of creation and availability of data that are relevant for road
safety purposes
• Benchmarking of the performance of data sharing mechanisms, lessons learnt, enablers, blocking
factors (aggregated/anonym ised, with due respect to confidentiality)
• Exchange and upgrade of knowledge on the availability and use of SRTI data (as defined and
regulated by the EU D R 886/2013) at the N APs
• Disseminate each other’s ecosystem and its working
• Jointly organise activities of common interest and invite each other when such participation would
be beneficial or appropriate
• The development of joint actions with the aim of improving the quality and accessibility of road
safety related data
• Intelligence and lessons learnt from each other’s data availability and sharing SRTI through the

5. The impact of emerging technologies on NAP operations
Considering the span of the last five years, several new technologies have been introduced in the field
of mobility, and transportation in general. Some proved very useful and expanded their usage, while
others remained only as pilot trials or are present only in very limited use cases. In th is context, it is
too early to identify any significant impact of such technologies for the NAPs or any other operational
aspects related to them. Therefore, this chapter briefly mentions examples of emerging technologies
and suggests potential impacts the y might have towards NAPs.
Autonomous vehicles as well as connected and automated vehicles are one of the new technologies
of the last years. They are highly promoted and supported both by policy frameworks and
manufacturers. They also come in different formats such as autonomous shuttles, robo -taxies,
autonomous delivery vehicles or private cars with automated and connected capabilities. The potential
impacts of all these vehicles for transport operations are:
• Create a need for new traffic management procedures :
Traffic management operations will need to consider the new mixed traffic situation and
account for the different needs of “human -driven” vehicles and the others. Difference s are
also between autonomous vehicles and connected vehicles, the latter being capable of
receiving direct instructions from a TMC. For example , METR ( Management of Electronic
Traffic Regulation) standards are being developed, which aim at defining a governance and
processes to generate trusted electronic traffic regulation.
• New Floating Car Data (FCD) sources:
The new vehicles will represent new sources of data, potentially better or enriched compared
to what is available from traditional vehicles. The use of FCD is already mentioned in the scope
of the new RTTI regulation.
• Create opportunities for dynamic rerouting :
Because connected vehicles can be given direct routing instructions, it might be possible to
reroute traffic much quicker and more reactive than the current situation.
• Create a need for more HD maps :
All autonomous or connected vehicles need high quality HD maps and as their numbers and
usage increases, the length of the road network for which those maps are available needs to
increase as well.
Internet of Things (IoT) is a technology that became increasingly used in all fields of activity, and it has
proven to be effective for many of them. In terms of NAPs or traffic information/management, there
might be an impact of IoT for a better and more comprehensive collection of traffic data. IoT traffic
sensors could be easier to deploy than conventional ones, and as such provide the opportunity to cover
more sections of the road network. On one hand, the information from them would be provided
through the NAPs, and on the other hand, based on this information, TMCs could be more effective.
Block chain is a technology known for many years, but it is still considered new. In the field of transport
it has limited applications, mostly in logistics for the exchange of electronic documents.
The latest new technology is Artificial Intelligence (AI) . Although promoted especially by generative AI
solutions, it has many other uses. In the field of transport, it proved to be useful for image processing
and it could become useful in TMCs for two use cases: a) improved and more reliable incident detection
and b) decision support tool for operators.
