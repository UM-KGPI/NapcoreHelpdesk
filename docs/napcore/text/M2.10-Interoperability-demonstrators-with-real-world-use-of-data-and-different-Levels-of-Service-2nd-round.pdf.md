1. Introduction
The second working group of the NAPCORE project (WG2 “Interoperability and level of service of
NAPs”) aims to define minimum conditions and coordinate the development of European National
Access Points (NAPs) by enhancing the ir compatibility and interoperability. Task 2.4 focuses on
enabling better cross-border interoperability and ensur ing the continuity of ITS Services. Task 2.4
supports those goals by developing NAP interoperability demonstrators.
An interoperability demonstrator is a digital solution that showcases and validates how different
systems, services or organizations can interact and exchange data. In the context of NAPs, these
demonstrations assess solutions for leveraging NAP data to facilitate mobility data exchange,
considering real-world scenarios from the data consumer’s perspective.
Task 2.4 involves two milestones, each corresponding to a round of interoperability demonstrators.
The first milestone (M2.6) describes an Interchange demonstrator for real-time, cross-border, Safety-
Related Traffic Information (SRTI). This document pertains to the second milestone (M2.10), which
includes the following demonstrators:
1. Interchange demonstrator (already covered in the first round)
2. Interoperable NAP demonstrator of a marketplace or clearing house NAP
3. Interoperable NAP demonstrator with Metadata
4. Alternative Fuels Demonstrator
These demonstrators were developed throughout the NAPCORE project and presented at the 2023
and 2024 Mobility Data Days in Budapest and Turin, respectively.

2. Context and background
2.1. National Access Points (NAPs)
The Directive 2010/40/EU on Intelligent Transport Systems (ITS) and its Delegated Regulations
mandate the establishment of a National Access Point (NAP) for mobility data within each European
Member State. Currently, there are over 30 operational National Access Points, spanning across nearly
all EU Member States and beyond, wherein mobility-related data is disseminated and made accessible
for purposes such as travel information services.

2.2. NAP Levels of Service (LoS)
While WG2 focuses on enhancing interoperability between NAPs as a whole, Task 2.1 (T2.1 ) aims to
harmonize and improve the Level of Service (LoS) of NAPs. To achieve this, T2.1 developed the NAP
LoS KPI Framework (NLKF), which evaluates the LoS of NAPs based on their features. Some of these
features are critical for the development of demonstrators, and additional features may be required
for their successful implementation. Moreover, the outcomes of the demonstrators may highlight the
need for further features to be incorporated into NAPs to foster interoperability and enhance mobility
data exchange through this platform. Consequently, during the development of the demonstrators,
task leads were asked to review the NLKF features and propose potential adaptations.
2.3. NAP Reference Architecture

system through several views: motivational, functional, physical, communications, organizational, and
security. Each view offers a different perspective, from the reasons behind the system's creation to
the interactions between i ts components and the relationships between actors. Based on the
European ITS Framework Architecture (FRAME) methodology, the NRA aims to harmonize the
functionalities and procedures of National Access Points (NAPs) across Europe.

3. Summary description of the demonstrators
3.1. Interchange demonstrator
Scope and objectives
The primary goal of this demonstrator is to show how service provider s can search and access safety
related traffic data from different countries in an easy way through a single arbitrary NAP. In addition,
the demonstrator aims at providing input for the NAP reference architecture and KPIs regarding level
of service.
The interchange is an implementation of the European C -ITS hybrid specification which is essentially
the definition of two APIs that enable both metadata sharing and distribution of actual data. The two
APIs are the Basic interface (BI) and the Improved interface (II), also called the federation layer . This
demonstrator describes why the use of the II and BI capabilities of the interchange, is a good way of
making it easy for service provider to find and access transport related data using the NAPs.
The overarching goal can be summarized as a use case: “As a Service Provider, I wish to provide real -
time safety related traffic information, cross-border, to my end users, so that they can receive relevant
and updated information in a timely fashion and plan their journey accordingly.”
This has been broken down into two more specific use cases:
Use case 1: “As a service provider I would like to have access to up-to-date meta-data from one source,
and the tools and mechanism for setting up necessary endpoints on the fly, so that the data I provide
is always updated and available for the end-users of my services.”
The expected outcome of the first use case is:
• An enhanced NAP model with an added Improved Interface (II) demonstrating an effective way
to distribute metadata amongst the NAPs.
• A near production -ready tool/interface for exploring the metadata in the NAP Network,
available as an open-source repository.
• By using the enhanced NAP model, the service providers can find a list of relevant metadata
and filtering criteria available.
• The NAPs operate in common federated network, so querying one NAP for its metadata will
return all metadata for all data sources made available by any country or organization that is
connected to the network, granted that the NAP has the control interface, defined as the II -
interface.
Use case 2: “As a service provider I would like to collect traffic-related messages on behalf of users from
one official source, relevant to his or her location and route, even when crossing national borders, which
enables the end-user to make decision regarding his and her route in ample time to reduce travel time
and avoid incidents.”
The second use case aims to show:
• That an enhance NAP model with an added BI -interface is a good solution for a standardized
and structured mode of creating, filtering, and accessing metadata and actual data.
• Functionality for generating publish/subscribe endpoints on the NAPs to receive data based
on needs and available data.
• Filtering mechanisms based on geographical position, message type or other criteria, to ensure
that the data is updated and relevant.

Overview of findings
The demonstration elicited a highly favourable response from Be -Mobile, who assumed the role as
outlined in the overarching use case : “As a Service Provider I wish to provide real time safety related
traffic information, cross border, to my end users, so that they can receive relevant and updated
information in a timely fashion and plan their journey accordingly.”
Be-Mobile found it easy to find the data they needed. Especially after retrieving data from one country,
it was very easy to add data from another country. There was no need to search elsewhere; rather,
they simply repeated the steps they had already completed.
The main takeaway from this demonstrator is that using concepts developed around the Interchange
in the C-ROADS and NordicWay projects, like the Improved and Basic interfaces, could provide several
benefits for the NAPs. This includes facilitating the discovery and utilization of transport-related data.
Furthermore, the federation removes the need for a complex web of bilateral agreements and
credentials across multiple organization and facilitate data sharing across organizations and borders.
By providing flexible filtering options, the concepts enable service providers to get exactly the data
their users need.
The demonstrator has provided the building blocks and concepts for creating both a tight and loose
coupling between the NAPs and the Interchange concept. To achieve tight integration, the NAP
requires an AMQP message broker or a publish/subscribe mechanism, both Basic and Improved
Interfaces, the demonstrator's REST-API, and the implementation of the created webpage. In contrast,
loose integration only requires access to an Interchange, hosting for the REST-API and webpage, and a
link to the webpage from the NAP. Due to time and cost constraints, the demonstrator could not build
a tightly coupled solution, but through a loosely coupled solution the project demonstrated that a
commercial service provider can quickly start receiving and using data.

3.2. Marketplace demonstrator
Scope and objectives
The Marketplace Demonstrator project represents an initiative to enhance the interoperability of
National Access Points (NAPs) across Member States. At its core, the project focuses on establishing
seamless cross -border data exchange between the Netherlands and Germany, u sing planned
roadworks data as a pilot case study to demonstrate the potential for broader European integration of
mobility data.
This demonstrator addresses a fundamental challenge in modern cross -border logistics and traffic
management. Currently, while data is available through various National Access Points, data
consumers face the cumbersome task of accessing multiple NAPs to ga ther similar datasets from
different countries. This fragmentation creates significant inefficiencies and poses substantial barriers
to effective cross-border operations, for example affecting logistics service providers who need to plan
efficient routes across European borders.
The demonstrator's primary objective is to showcase a marketplace that enables secure and efficient
data exchange between Dutch data consumers and German data providers. By implementing a central

The demonstrator's architecture builds upon proven components from the Catena-X automotive data
space, specifically leveraging the Eclipse Tractus -X open -source project, which has demonstrated
success in connecting over 180 participants. This architectural approach combines robust security with
scalable performance through a multi-layered design that ensures both data protection and efficient
access.
At the foundation of the system lies the Eclipse Data Space Connector (EDC), which is divided into two
main components: the Control Plane for managing access control, policy enforcement, and contract
negotiations, and the Data Plane for handling the actual secure data transfer between participants.
This core component works in concert with a comprehensive Identity and Access Management system
through Keycloak, while a Managed Identity Wallet handles credential management. A central
catalogue, hosted outside the dataspace, facilitates dataset discovery, while a sophisticated data
transformation pipeline converts German DATEX II data to the Dutch OTM format every 12 hours,
ensuring regular updates and compatibility across different national standards.
The architecture implements distinct functional layers to ensure smooth operation. The Application
Layer manages metadata, data storage, and transformation processes, while the Data Exchange Layer
oversees secure data transfer between participants. A dedic ated Security Layer provides
comprehensive identity and access management, all built upon an Infrastructure Layer using
Kubernetes, an open-source container orchestration platform that automates the deployment, scaling,
and management of container -based applications. This layered approach ensures that each
component can be maintained and upgraded independently, while maintaining the overall system's
integrity.

Overview of findings
The implementation of the demonstrator has yielded valuable insights across multiple dimensions of
cross-border data sharing. From a technical implementation perspective, the use of established opensource tools like Catena -X and Tractus -X significantly ac celerated the development process. The
solution demonstrated a strong potential for scalability and flexibility, with an architecture designed
to accommodate future additions of participants and services while maintaining robust security
protocols throughout the system.
However, the project also revealed significant standardization challenges that need to be addressed
for future implementations. Notable differences exist in how Germany and the Netherlands implement
DATEX II, which complicated the direct conversion of data between systems. The absence of an
established standard for OTM5 necessitated making certain assumptions about the correct data
structure. These differences underscore the pressing need for further standardization efforts in crossborder data exchange, particularly as more countries join similar initiatives.
Several key factors emerged as critical to the project's success. The use of standardized components
like EDC proved instrumental in supporting interoperability between different sectors and regions. The
modular architecture demonstrated its value by allowing for organic growth and adap tation to
emerging needs. Additionally, the implementation of strong security measures and clear governance
structures proved essential for maintaining trust within the data space.

3.3. Metadata demonstrator
Scope and objectives
Metadata, a detailed and standardized data set description, facilitates access by enabling
discoverability of data sets within a NAP. Metadata describes the administration, organisation, and
content of a dataset and of a data service. Metadata datasets are therefore crucial elements to make
NAPs accessible and searchable. The primary aim of this demonstrator was to demonstrate the
feasibility of metadata consolidation from multiple National Access Points (NAPs) into a unified
metadata portal.
Similar to the Interchange demonstrator, this work was based in an overall use case, where a service
provider wishes to view metadata of all NAPs to get a grasp of the available traffic data characteristics.
This was broken down into two more specific use cases:
Use case 1: OVERVIEW OF NAP DATASETS – “As a Service Provider I would like to grasp which countries
have an easily available metadata description of the dataset, organized by geographical area, so that I
can design a product that is capable of extracting data across borders.”
Use case 2: FILTER THE AVAILABLE DATA BY CATEGORY – “As a Service Provider I would like to have
access to up -to-date metadata that is organized according to a certain category, for example Heavy
Vehicle Parking, so that I can observe what are the characteristics of that dataset (format of the data,
updated timeframe).”
The Demonstrator aimed to show how heterogeneous metadata from individual NAPs can be
harvested, processed and displayed in an integrated IT solution. The main functionality is the
accessibility of metadata from multiple NAPs, i.e., a data consumer would be able to discover NAP data
offerings from multiple NAPs via one online platform. This way, the solution is somewhat similar to the
EU Open Data portal, which also harvests several other data portals.
Description of the system’s architecture
The Demonstrator, as an application, is divided into three modules: Extractor, Processor, and Display.

Limitations of the demonstrator
One notable limitation of the Demonstrator stems from the time latency observed during the
extracting and processing phases. This latency hinders real -time updates of metadata datasets,
creating a lag between the availability of new data and its incorporat ion into the Demonstrator.
Addressing this delay is crucial to ensure that the Demonstrator reflects the most current and relevant
information.
The Demonstrator focused on two modules for metadata extraction. While effective for extracting
metadata from a single file encompassing all NAP datasets and using CKAN API to obtain and process
datasets, this limited scope may overlook other potential sources. Expanding the rang e of extraction
modules would enhance the Demonstrator's adaptability to diverse data structures.
Additionally, the ease of scaling the Demonstrator, particularly beyond the proof -of-concept (POC)
stage, is contingent upon well -documented and readily available metadata extraction APIs. A robust
extraction API ensures scalability, allowing the Demonstra tor to adapt to an expanding ecosystem of
NAPs seamlessly.
The registration process introduces additional steps when extracting metadata, an aspect not utilized
in this POC. Moreover, the difference in the extraction processes between data and metadata from
the same datasets raises considerations for streamlining these operations to enhance efficiency.
Limitations of the NAPs involved
The lack of harmonized metadata across NAPs introduces challenges during the integration process.
Field-by-field validation becomes a necessity, especially when dealing with disparate formats. The
adoption of the DCAT-AP format has proven advantageous, sim plifying the identification of gaps and
facilitating a more standardized approach to metadata integration.
Still, even a DCAT-AP-compliant representation of metadata in a NAP does not resolve all challenges.
The main reason is the heterogeneous provision of metadata information across individual NAPs, in
particular, the population and usage of individual metadata fields. For example, some NAPs describe
the data contents by a free-text description; other NAPS use a (sometimes complex) set of keywords,
and others use a predefined list of content categories. The mobilityDCAT-AP specification is resolving
such issue s by clear stipulations on the population and usage of metadata fields. Thus, it is
recommended that any future deployment of new NAP systems or revisions of existing NAP systems
consider a native implementation of the mobilityDCAT -AP specification within their internal
information models, so a post -mapping (as proposed in the Demonstrator architecture) becomes
reluctant.
To overcome these NAP limitations, the outcomes of this demonstrator were used to provide feedback
to the other NAPCORE tasks mentioned in the section 2. Concerning the NLKF, different KPIs were
deemed crucial for the implementation of the demonstrator. NAPs participating in this demonstration
were required to meet a specific Level of Service for features within three key categories: 1 (Access),
3 (Data Discovery), and 7 (Operation Policy). Additionally, aspects of the NAP Reference Architecture—
encompassing the functional, physical, and organizational layers —were identified as critical to the
activities carried out by the demonstrators. These aspects are described in detail in Annex B.

4. Conclusions
Looking at the demonstrators’ individual reports (Annexes A, B, and C), as well as the highlights added
to this document, it was possible to extract some common lessons from the different solutions
adopted. Those refer primarily to the importance of specific features and common challenges faced
and are included in the subchapter 4.1. The individual reports also contained specific sections or
mentions of features and functionalities included in the NLKF and NRA; those were analysed , and a
summary of the feedback already provided by the demonstrators to these NAPCORE tools has been

4.1. Common lessons learnt and challenges faced
The adoption of the MobilityDCAT-AP, the NAPCORE-recommended metadata specification, has been
identified as a key value -add by three out of four demonstrators. The Interchange demonstrator
highlights that incorporating this specification into future iterations of the interchange solution could
bring significant improvements. Meanwhile, the Metadata and AF demonstrators underscore that
adopting a common catalogue standard significantly enhances data discoverability, streamlining the
locating and sharing of NAP information.
Standardization issues emerged as a critical challenge during development. In the MarketPlace
demonstrator, one of the most time-consuming tasks was establishing the "marketplace" between the
German and Dutch NAPs. The difficulty lay in translating the German DATEX II into the Dutch DATEX II
format, underscoring interoperability hurdles. Similarly, processing AF data within the AF
demonstrator also presented substantial challenges due to standardization gaps. Most data was either
in proprietary formats or adhered to the OCPI standard, making it difficult to process or convert into
DATEX II, the recommended standard for AF data types.
Open-source tools played a pivotal role in the design of various solutions. In the MarketPlace
implementation, tools such as Catena-X and Tractus-X significantly accelerated the development of the
data space. Likewise, the Metadata and AF demonstrators relied on the CKAN framework to efficiently
find and process datasets from multiple NAPs, demonstrating its utility in handling different
information sources.
Some limitations of the current NAPs’ setup were also observed. For instance, the current features
offered by most NAPs are insufficient to support the tight integration suggested by the Interchange
demonstrator, meaning that implementing an interchange solution would require additional
functionalities within the NAP framework. The Metadata demonstrator further illustrated that issue,
observing that several NAPs lack crucial features such as the harmonised description of datasets and
the difficulty of extracting metadata in a machine-to-machine readable format.
Scalability remains a mixed prospect across demonstrators. Both the MarketPlace and Metadata
solutions showcased strong scalability potential, reflecting their robust design. However, the AF
demonstrator raised concerns regarding scalability, particularly in data management. It emphasized
that handling large volumes of data could introduce significant challenges, potentially limiting the
solution's effectiveness in larger-scale applications.

4.3. Combined feedback to the NAP Reference Architecture
All demonstrators incorporated an architectural approach in their design, as evidenced by their
detailed reports. These reports reference overall concepts, requirements, functional views, and
methodological approaches. The NAP Reference Architecture (NRA), presented in milestone M2.9,
included a fully developed functional view and partially developed physical and organizational views,
while communications and security views were not addressed. These views provide perspective when
setting up the minimum NAP, particularly leveraged by the metadata and AFIR demonstrators, which
built on the metadata directory functionality.
The metadata and AFIR demonstrators provided feedback on key components in the functional,
physical, and organizational views used during their development, highlighting the need for further
clarification on "metadata quality checks." The NAP reference architecture is crucial for building NAPs,
as demonstrated by these two projects.
Although no direct feedback was provided via the NRA commenting template, useful insights were
inferred from the demonstrators' reports, leading to a better-structured NAP reference architecture.
The architecture also serves as a blueprint for interfaces and specifications, allowing for the description
of best practices. Even though as no direct feedback identifying specific standards (apart from
mobilityDCAT-AP), profiles, or best practices was provided, d emonstrators made numerous
architectural decisions regarding software use, security levels, protocols, data standards, and interface
definitions. Key experiences include:
• Using mobilityDCAT-AP for describing and harvesting data.
• Prescribing a harmonized API across NAPs with guidelines.
• Exploring C-ROADS interfaces with pub-sub mechanisms.
• Recognizing that using the same standard does not guarantee interoperability; guidelines and
profiles should be referenced if they exist.
