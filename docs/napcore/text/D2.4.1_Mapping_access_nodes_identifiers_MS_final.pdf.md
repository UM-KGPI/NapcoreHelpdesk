Abstract
This report, "Mapping of the Access Nodes Identifiers in the Member States" , constitutes a
deliverable of the NAPCORE project, a Programme Support Action funded under the Connecting
Europe Facility (CEF). It aims to facilitate EU-wide coordination of National Access Points (NAPs) for
the harmonized implementation of European specifications on the ITS Directive (2010/40/EU) ,
particularly focusing on Multimodal Travel Information Services (MMTIS).
Within the framework of NAPCORE Task 2.4, the current report presents findings which were collected
by structured interviews based on a survey conducted across 21 EU Member States , as well as
Norway, Switzerland and the United Kingdom . Additionally, key stakeholders such as MobilityData,
the European Union Agency for Railways (ERA), and the Airports Council International (ACI Europe)
were interviewed. The survey assesses the current state of digital identification systems for transport
access nodes , defined as pre-defined locations where passengers may board or leave scheduled
transport or transport on demand, and explores opportunities for further harmonization in alignment
with EU Directive (EU) 2023/2661.
The harmonization of access node identifiers contributes to seamless multimodal travel in the EU.
While progress has been made, disparities in implementation and fragmented data standards persist.
By leveraging existing frameworks , strengthening governance and collaboration , and expanding
coverage to emerging mobility services , the EU can achieve a unified, interoperable system that
enhances passenger experience , operational efficiency , and sustainable mobility . Future efforts
under NAPCORE and EU directive s must prioritize execution, stakeholder engagement , and
continuous improvement to realize this vision. Such policy recommendations will be delivered in the
second deliverable of Task 2.4 of NAPCORE which is due April 2026.

1. Introduction
This report is the first outcome of the Task 2.4, EU IDs for Multimodal Access Nodes of NAPCORE, a
Programme Support Action - funded under the Connecting Europe Facility (CEF) 1 – which gathers
public authorities of all EU members states and which aims at facilitating EU wide coordination of NAPs

adopted in the framework of the ITS Directive.

NAPCORE for what relates to Multimodal Travel Information Services (MMTIS).

is addressed in this report, related to Task 2.4, EU IDs for Multimodal Access Nodes, part of the Working

2. Background and Methodology
2.1. Background
This task supports the European Commission objective to coordinate the National Access Points (NAPs)
and National Bodies (NBs) established under the Directive 2010/40/EU3, so-called ITS Directive, on the
framework for the deployment of Intelligent Transport Systems in the field of road transport and for
interfaces with other modes of transport.
The task is developed in application of the Delegated Regulation of the ITS Directive addressing
“Multimodal Travel Information Services” , in short MMTIS DR 2. The connected task is part of the
working programme for Directive 2010/40/EU for the period 2024-2028.
In particular, the task aims to map existing tools in place to identify access nodes and review the
current availability of access nodes identifiers at national or European level.
The Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/1926 defines access nodes as follows:
‘Access node’ means a pre-defined location where passengers may board or leave scheduled
transport or transport on demand3.

related to:

2.2. Methodology
To better understand the current situation across Europe, UITP conducted structured interviews with
representatives from different countries. The complete interview format is included in Annex 1 of this
document.
Format: 1- hour video conference meeting
Content: A questionnaire of 10 questions
Goal: To gather insights on how each country approaches the digital identification and registration of
transport access nodes (e.g. stations, stops) and identify needs and gaps.
The key topics addressed are:
• Existence and scope of harmonized digital ID systems
• Modes of transport covered
• Responsible authorities
• Data structure, standards and access
• Regulatory or policy framework
• Challenges and outlook
2.2.1. Structured interview questions for Member States representatives
(1) In your country, is there any harmonized system in place (or being developed) for the digital
identification of transport access points (such as stations, stops)?
(2) Is there just one system or are there different systems existing in parallel, perhaps at different levels
(local, regional, national)?
(3) What modes of transport are covered by the register? (E.g. train, bus, urban rail, micromobility, ondemand, maritime, aviation, etc.)
(4) Who is in charge of this digital register/identification system?
(5) How is the digital register/identification system organized? ( e.g.What does a digital ID look like,
how is it composed? Are there sub-levels to identify different areas or even single bus stops/platforms
within the larger stations?)
(6) What data attributes are recorded for each access point? ( e.g.geographical coordinates (GPS or
other), infrastructure details, accessibility features, etc.) Which standards do you use?
(7) How is this data made available to different stakeholders? How can others access it? (Is the data
open and publicly accessible, or does it require agreements/licenses?)
(8) Are there any national regulations or policies that require or encourage the harmonization of ID for
access nodes? Which ones? Or has the harmonization been a sector initiative (without policies)?
(9) What are the main challenges faced in maintaining and improving this system? How do you see this
system evolving in the coming years?
(10) Is there anything else you consider relevant for understanding the digital registration of transport
access points?

Based on the information gathered, it is possible to identify that s ubstantial progress has been made
in recent years toward the definition of national access node IDs and the digitalisation of access nodes.
This includes the development of national guidelines, directives, and pilot projects for standardization,
which have established clear procedures and responsibilities at national level. As a result, reliance on
paper-based timetables has decreased, service integration has improved, and data accessibility
through NAPs has increased.
However, countries have varying levels of progress in implementing digital IDs for access nodes. While
some lack a national ID system, others allow Public Transport Operators (PTOs) to name stops without
standardized guidance. A few countries have launched pilot projects in collaboration with Public
Transport Authorities (PTAs), and some have established robust systems with we ll-defined, regularly
updated online guidelines. This disparity highlights opportunities for further development and
harmonization.
Regarding the ITS Directive 2010/40/EU, Annex III, data type 4, static and dynamic accessibility
information for access nodes remains largely uncovered. Expanding this data , such as barrier -free
routes for different disabilities and real -time updates on mechanical components ( e.g. elevators,
escalators) could significantly enhance the user experience during trips.
Projects as DATA4PT5 have contributed to increase the published static data in NeTEx format and realtime data on SIRI. As well, the first phase of NAPCORE6 has developed an ITS Data Dictionary to create
a common understanding on data categories, and a first metadata catalogue specification for mobility
data platforms to enable interoperable discoverability of data.
Despite the previous efforts, the i mplementation of Transmodel and associated standards remains
limited to countries with well-established ITS deployment in public transport. The dedicated efforts on
multimodal mobility of the second phase of NAPCORE provides an opportunity to broaden the use of
European standards for timetables (NeTEx) and dynamic information (SIRI), promoting greater
consistency across systems.
An element of Transmodel V6 is the IFOPT (Identification of Fixed Objects in Public Transport), which
is a CEN Technical Specification that defines a Reference Data Model for describing key fixed public

Information was provided by IMT (Instituto da Mobilidade e dos Transportes). Currently, there is no
harmonised national system for identifying transport access points. There is no national ID assigned to
all stops, and the definitions and processes for registering new ones are not standardised. However,
nearly all public transport services are digitised and integrated into a single national system. While the
data lacks a fully standardised format, all stops are digitally recorded and accessible through the NAP.
The MMTIS is supported by the national system for public transport data (STePP) available online. Each
component is managed by a different department within the same national authority.
STePP is a GIS -based platform designed to comply with Regulation (EC) No 1370/2007 and support
market opening created through law 52/2015 which did not include requirements for standardisation
of IDs. STePP includes static information for all regular public transport modes (train, bus, domestic
coach, road and inland waterways). It covers some DRT bus services and does not include parking,
micromobility, maritime or aviation modes. It includes financial and contractual data and involves 23
regional and 50 municipal authorities.
The NAP, introduced later, focuses on road and multimodal transport, it is the only source of real-time
information. It includes regular public transport (bus and rail), the largest bike -sharing systems, and
parking information . Information is available online without registration. The NAP is managed by
various departments, with the planning department handling multimodal data and the infrastructure
department managing other ITS-related data.
Both systems serve different purposes and are essential, but there is a strong push to enhance
interoperability and data exchange between them using NeTEx format . Harmonisation remains a
challenge, i.e. all stops are included but lack consistent national ID assignments. Full integration is
difficult due to differences in purpose and content, but alignment is a clear objective.
The PTAs are responsible to update the information available in STePP. PTOs are legally required to
input their service data into the system and PTAs are responsible for validating the data. Noncompliance by PTOs can lead to fines. The information flow is presented below.

3.2.19. Romania
The company "Transport Public" in Romania provided the information. Currently, there is no

3.2.21. Spain
Information was provided by the ministry of transport, entity in charge of the MMTIS NAP. In Spain

3.2.22. Sweden
Information provided by Samtrafiken. Sweden has a database called the Swedish Stop Registry (SHR),
managed and maintained by Samtrafiken . The SHR consolidates data contributed and updated by all
PTAs and PTOs according to local planning and infrastructure changes . It assigns unique national IDs
to each stop, addressing issues like duplicated or conflicting IDs across regions. The Swedish Transport
Agency (Transportstyrelsen) oversees compliance, under regulation TSFS 2012:2.
The SHR has four main objectives:
• Standardization: Ensure unique, national IDs for each stop.
• Accessibility: Allow consistent and traceable access to stop data across Sweden.
• Multimodal platforms: Facilitate seamless combined travel, such as Resplus, enabling ticket
integration.
• Efficiency: Reduce redundant local databases and manual reconciliation.
The database includes bus stops, train stations, station areas, and transfer points. It does not include
micromobility and other modes, but it has the potential to include it in accordance with national and
MMTIS regulation. The following information is included in the SHR for each stop, according to the
MMTIS regulation16.
• Stop name
• Stop ID number (national and local)
• Geographical coordinates
• Municipality code
• Minimum transfer time

3.3.3. Airports Council International (ACI)
Major commercial airports (around 500 in Europe) use the IATA 3 -letter codes for passenger-facing
systems, including flight bookings, baggage tags, and travel itineraries. These codes are embedded in
global distribution systems and are familiar to travelers, airlines, and ground handlers.
The remaining airports —often smaller regional or general aviation airfields —rely on ICAO 4 -letter
codes, maintained by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO). These codes are used for
operational and regulatory purposes, such as flight planning and air traffic control. While IATA and
ICAO maintain correlation tables to bridge these two systems, the lack of a unified identifier creates
challenges for multimodal integration, where passengers may need to transition seamlessly between
air, rail, and road transport.
The European Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) provides the most comprehensive dataset for airports, as
it is responsible for certifying and regulating airfields across Europe. EASA’s data includes runway
orientations, threshold locations, and terminal certific ations. This data is pilot -centric to ensure the
safe operation of aircraft, not to facilitate passenger movements or multimodal connections (terminal
gates, check-in counters, or connections to rail and bus stations). The data by the EASA is not directly
related with the IATA or ICAO codes.

3.4. Similarities and common ground
3.4.1. Existence of harmonised systems
The implementation of access node identifiers varies across countries. In recent years, progress has
been made toward digitizing access node information and identifiers (e.g. Portugal, Spain). However,
each country has adopted its own approach, shaped by its unique context and collaboration among
NAPs.
Some cooperation has arisen in recent years between NAPs contributing to local harmonization. This
is the case of the Nordic NeTEx profile created by Norway and in use by Sweden and Finland, the use
of NaPTAN by UK and Ireland, and the use of IFOPT by Germany and Austria.
3.4.1.1. Modes covered
Most ID systems focus on public transport modes, including buses, metros, trams, and trains. Other
services such as ferries , cable-cars or DRT are sometimes included if they are part of the public
transport local offer. However, touristic, unscheduled, or DRT services are often excluded, depending
on local policies and obligations to report this data.
Micromobility services typically remain outside these systems, with data shared only at the local level
between operators and cities according to local agreements between the operators and the cities .
Airports are partially included, with incomplete or fragmented information about terrestrial modes
that connect the airport according to the services that connect the airport, and exceptionally with
information about the flights (e.g. Spain). This remains an opportunity to include further modes in the
system.

Countries have various approaches to assigning IDs for multimodal access nodes—such as airports and

ridesharing, parking, and shared mobility services like bike -sharing, car -sharing, and scooters. This

data standards applied to each transport mode. For example, airports typically use internal ID systems
for gates, terminals, and transport stops, while train stations often rely on distinct national or regional
ID systems for platforms and connections. The lack of compatibility among these systems leads to
fragmented identification, management, and communication of access nodes for both passengers and
service providers.
Access nodes IDs in other Member States remain a key concern among NAPs and stakeholders
interviewed. Issues include unclear maintenance responsibilities, inconsistent update frequencies, and
compatibility problems between systems. Lack of documentation on the identification procedure and
update frequency leads to lack of interoperability, due to duplicate IDs or lost references to services,
especially when IDs change over time or vary in format.
Interoperability remains an unresolved goal in the current deployment of access node IDs. Some of the
countries interviewed lack cross-country, and sometimes even intra-country compatibility. Achieving
interoperability would support initiatives like the Eu ropean Mobility Dataspace and simplify user
interfaces. The European governance established for rail with the European Union Agency for railways
(ERA) responsible as system authority for the management and assignment of EU -IDs for rail-related
datasets accessible via NAPs could also be a role model for other transport mode benefitting from the
service of an EU agency.
In some cases, access node IDs include information such as geographical location, transport mode,
administrative division, or even transport operator within the ID which leads to disruptions and
duplicates. Most systems have inalterability of IDs ensuring consistency in referencing access nodes,
regardless of operational shifts.
IDs for deactivated stops are in some cases retained to avoid conflicts with new stops. While marked
as "inactive," these IDs preserve historical data and prevent misalignment in system references.

5. Outlook and conclusions
The landscape of access node identifiers in Europe reflects both significant progress and persistent
challenges. While digitization of access node information —evident in Portugal and Spain —reflects a
positive trend toward standardization and interoperability, the diversity of national approaches,
shaped by local contexts and collaboration among National Access Points (NAPs), continues to create
fragmentation.
Each country maintains its own identifiers for access nodes, timetables, and services, but these are
commonly not aligned or cross-referenced across borders or even between domestic operators. This
lack of cohesion makes more difficult to aggregate or exchange information effectively, leaving
datasets in silos and in some cases the same physical access node represented by different IDs
depending on the data provider, region, or transport mode. The mapping presented in this document
identified opportunities to include additional involvement of stakeholders within the fragmented
landscape, including PTOs, PTAs, the railway and aviation sector, and alternative mobility providers.
Improving the accessibility of information presents an opportunity to streamline the ID process for
access nodes. Detailed data on stops and stations , such as stairs, elevators, and escalators , is critical
for identifying barrier-free routes. On this regard, information provided by the ERSAD by the ERA will
contribute to identify the accessibility of rail services. Similarly, information available via the NAP in
The Netherlands, or Norway about accessibility and characteristics of information can contribute to
identify how to gather, maintain and communicate accessibility information.
Defining roles and responsibilities among stakeholders is vital for effective ID management,
particularly in multimodal hubs. Collaboration among stakeholders is required to harmonize existing
IDs and address overlaps or inconsistencies. Ensuring their voluntary participation and alignment with
regulations is crucial for successful implementation and long -term compliance. Information sharing
and organization of workshops for peer-to-peer learnings across NAPs will be considered in NAPCORE,
including share of experiences after stablishing stops registries that can contribute to the development
of these systems in other Member States.
Cross-border access nodes remain a challenge, requiring additional coordination between NAPs and
stakeholders. Austria’s inclusion of border-access nodes serves as a model for addressing this issue.
Some of the common characteristics identified in the different systems in place include uniqueness,
traceability, and interoperability of IDs at national level, these IDs are usually unique across all access
nodes, and they remain in place even if the access node gets deactivated, to prevent duplication.

Most systems analyzed include public transport services, such as buses, trams, railways, metros, and

alliances, leading to more connected services and shared information. Further research by UITP could

integrate them effectively into the broader public transport ecosystem.
The harmonization of access node identifiers across the EU will contribute to achieve seamless
multimodal travel. The current state of access node identifiers in Europe reflects a dynamic but uneven
landscape. While progress has been made in digitizing and standardizing these identifiers, significant
challenges remain —particularly in achieving interoperability, expand ing coverage to include all
transport modes, and ensuring consistent data quality and maintenance.
The level of advancement has been influenced by e ach country’s context and regulatory framework .
This has led to a variety of ID systems, which, while tailored to local needs, could lack compatibility
with one another.
Some initiatives like the Nordic NeTEx profile used by Norway, Finland and Switwerland and being
regarded as a potential example to follow by other NAPs, the NaPTAN used by the UK and Ireland, or
the IFOPT system used by Germany and Austria demonstrate how regional collaboration can drive
harmonization. Scaling these efforts could bridge gaps between countries and modes.
Several technical and governance challenges persist, including t he complexity of multimodal hubs,
inconsistent ID formats, and unclear maintenance responsibilities . This highlights the need for a
harmonized approach and future guidelines on responsibilities and governance frameworks.
There have been both top-down legislative approaches (e.g., Norway, Germany) and bottom -up
voluntary initiatives (e.g., Denmark, the Netherlands) that have proven effective. This indicates that a
tailor-made approach according to the national characteristics could lead to similar results . Different
countries are in the process of transposing the ITS Directive which could lead to an opportunity to
include specific requirements for access nodes IDs (e.g. Finland, Poland).
Currently, the absence of harmonized identifiers for critical data elements—such as access nodes and
timetable data—limits the functionality of national datasets and cross-border travel services. This
fragmentation is further exacerbated by the lack of standardized NeTEx and SIRI profiles, which
complicates data exchange and interoperability. As a result, passengers face inconsistencies in travel
information, while transport authorities and operators struggle to integrate services across borders
or even within their own networks.
Ultimately, compliance is not the goal in itself, but a criterion to support a future-proof mobility
ecosystem. By standardizing access node IDs, it could be possible to further develop common
European Mobility Data Spaces and increase smoother data exchange, improve real-time travel
information, and pave the way for innovative services. The next steps must focus on a better
understanding, increased execution, collaboration, consistent objectives and compatible roadmaps
between continuous improvement to define common EU-iDs ensuring the multiple stakeholders' role
of access nodes for the passenger sustainable mobility owning, holding and managing stop IDs data.
The next report shall address the issues presented and provide recommendations of a EU-wide ID for
access nodes.
